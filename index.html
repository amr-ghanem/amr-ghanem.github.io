The work

The gap I work in

A code check tells you a building complies. It does not tell you what the building will actually do when the ground moves, or when water arrives. Those are different questions, and the distance between them is where I spend my time.

Fragility analysis is how you close it. Instead of a pass or fail, you get a probability: given this intensity of shaking, how likely is this structure to reach this state of damage? That number is what lets an owner, a city, or a transport agency make a decision rather than a guess.

Cairo to Honolulu

I trained as a structural engineer in Egypt and spent two years designing steel — sugar refineries, a steel rolling mill, cement plants — before coordinating BIM across disciplines on projects where the model mattered more than the drawings.

Then I moved to Hawaiʻi for a PhD, and the problem changed shape. Islands concentrate the question. The same building may need to survive an earthquake, a tsunami, and a hurricane, and the standards treating each hazard separately do not tell you what happens when you consider them together.

Research and practice, at once

I now design and analyse structures at a firm whose president chairs the ASCE committee behind the tsunami provisions my research addresses. That is an unusual place to stand. You learn which parts of a standard are awkward to apply long before you write about them, and which research questions actually reach a drawing board.

The software matters too. Fragility pipelines, deep-learning crack detection, design automation — two of which became competition-winning ventures at UH. For analysis at this scale the tooling is not a side interest; it is the only way the work gets done at all.
